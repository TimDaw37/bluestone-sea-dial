Bluestone sea dial

Set how much it costs to move a load one kilometre by water, relative to one kilometre over flat ground, and the model finds the cheapest route from the Preseli outcrops to Stonehenge across real terrain. It is a test of assumptions, not a reconstruction of what happened.

Where the route switches

Share of the route on water as the water cost rises, with every other dial held where it is now. The switch is the first water cost at which the route is inland-preferring (water ≤ 10 % of its length).

How the cost works

The terrain is OS Terrain 50 averaged onto a 400 m or 200 m grid. Each move to a neighbouring cell is charged:

overland d + c × climb + 0.65c × descent on water m × d transfer + T at every land↔water step

Here d is the horizontal distance in metres, c is the climb penalty and m the water cost. With the steepest-slope setting at “Any” the overland charge depends only on how far and how much up and down, not on how steep. A 50 m climb costs the same up a cliff as up a long ramp. Setting a maximum slope closes the steeper moves.

Sea is every cell where most Terrain 50 posts are below 0 m OD or there are no posts offshore. The cheapest route is found with Dijkstra’s algorithm, run in your browser.

Reading the result

  • With the protocol settings (no transfer cost, climb penalty 4) the switch sits close to m ≈ 1.03. Sea wins when moving a load on water costs about the same as on flat ground or less. The sea and land routes are almost the same length, so the terrain contributes only a few per cent.
  • Without a transfer cost, the “inland” winner at the switch keeps to the south Wales coastal plain, crosses estuaries, and ferries over the Severn from Cardiff to Weston-super-Mare.
  • Make water dearer (2 to 10 × flat ground) and the route follows the Tywi to Llandeilo, crosses the coalfield valleys to Newport and takes a 3.5 km ferry over the Severn at Beachley–Aust.
  • Charge 25 km per embark or landing and the inland route avoids water altogether. It follows the Tywi to Llandovery, Brecon and Monmouth, and goes round the head of the Severn estuary. The switch falls to m ≈ 0.8.
  • Keeping within sight of land changes nothing, and limits of 20 or 10 km leave the switch where it is. Only a 2 km limit lowers it, to about 0.95.
  • Very cheap water sends the route round Land’s End. That route needs water at about a twentieth of the flat-ground cost.

What the model leaves out

  • Tides, currents, weather, seasons and the risk of losing a stone.
  • How the stones were carried: no sledges, boats, rafts or crew sizes.
  • Vegetation, woodland and wetland. Terrain 50 is today’s surface.
  • Mid-Holocene sea level. By c. 3000 BC relative sea level here was already within a few metres of present, not the tens of metres of the early Holocene. The coasts these routes use are mostly steep, so a few metres of vertical change barely moves the shoreline at Terrain 50 grain. The low Severn levels near Avonmouth and Weston are the exception, where the tidal edge may have lain some way inland of today’s.
  • Any claim that the cheapest route is the one used.
